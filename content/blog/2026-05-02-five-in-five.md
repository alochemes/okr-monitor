The standard way most companies write Key Results is wrong, and the wrongness is fixable in five minutes.

Here is the standard way. The Chief of Staff or the head of strategy schedules a half-day OKR retreat. Leadership shows up. They start with the company strategy. They debate framing for thirty minutes. They write a candidate Objective on the whiteboard. Then someone — usually the most senior person in the room — proposes a candidate Key Result. The room reacts. The KR gets edited live. Forty-five minutes later, the room has agreed on something that nobody is in love with but nobody objects to.

Then they do this again, three more times, because there are three more Objectives. By minute 240, energy is low and the KRs reflect it. The retreat ends. The KRs go in the doc. Three months later, the retro starts with someone saying, "honestly we kind of forgot about KR-3 by week six."

This sequence has a single failure mode and it is identifiable. **The KRs were proposed live, in front of leadership, by people performing for each other.** Live group writing produces Key Results that are safe — defensible, modest, easy to commit to in front of the boss. Safe Key Results are an excellent way to feel aligned and a terrible way to drive change.

We use a different exercise. It takes five minutes and does most of the work the four-hour retreat does, with one critical difference: nobody writes in front of anybody.

## The exercise

The exercise has four rounds.

**Round 1 — five minutes.** Pick one Objective. Set a timer. Each person in the room — separately, no looking at each other's drafts, no debating, no editing — writes 5 candidate Key Results for that Objective. Fast and rough. Bad ones welcomed. The point is volume, not quality.

**Round 2 — ten minutes.** Read aloud, person by person. Cluster duplicates. The room produces 8–15 unique candidates from a group of four people doing 5 each. Most candidates are versions of three or four underlying ideas; that is fine.

**Round 3 — five minutes.** Vote. Each person picks their top three. Tally the votes.

**Round 4 — fifteen minutes.** Take the top three by vote. Pressure-test each against six rules (more on the rules in a moment). If a top-voted KR fails a rule, fix it on the spot. If it can't be fixed, drop it and pick the next-highest vote-getter.

That's the whole exercise. Forty minutes total when you include the read-aloud and the pressure test. You walk out with three Key Results that the room voted into existence — which means people own them — written in the canonical shape that a real measurement system can read.

## Why the five-minute timer matters

The five-minute solo timer is doing the heavy lifting. Without it, the exercise collapses into the OKR retreat. Here is why.

When you have unlimited time to write a Key Result with leadership watching, you write the *defensible* one. The one you could justify if challenged. The one you are 80% sure you can hit. That is exactly the wrong target. A Key Result you are 80% sure you can hit isn't a stretch — it's a project status update.

Five minutes is short enough that you stop self-editing. You write the Key Result you actually believe is true and you don't have time to soften it. The bad ones — the ones you'd never propose in a meeting — slip through. They are usually the most useful ones. *"Cancel two thirds of our Monday meetings."* *"Reduce the team by 15%."* *"Get cited in three RevOps newsletters next month."* These are real candidate KRs people have written in 5-in-5 sessions. None of them would survive a live group debate. All of them have been adopted (at least once, by at least one company we've talked to) when the timer surfaced them.

## The six rules — pressure-testing the survivors

After the vote, each finalist gets pressure-tested against these six. If it fails any, fix it or drop it.

1. **Numeric.** Number, unit, deadline. No exceptions.
2. **Observable in a system you already have.** If measuring it requires a new survey or a new tool, you'll stop measuring it by week 4.
3. **Outcome metric, not vanity.** Pageviews are not a KR. Activations are, but only if "activation" has a definition.
4. **One number per KR.** No "ship X **and** improve Y by 20%" — half-success looks like full-failure.
5. **At most 5 KRs per Objective.** More than 5 means you're listing tasks.
6. **No anti-patterns.** "100% of customers respond to NPS" is a process metric. "Improve quality" is a KPI. "Ship feature X" is a task.

If your top-voted finalist passes all six, you're done. Most don't pass on the first try — the most common failure is missing baseline. ("NPS reaches 50" — okay, what's the baseline? If you don't know, that's not a KR yet, that's a target without context.)

Reset the timer to five minutes for the next Objective. Do it three more times. You now have three Objectives with three to five real Key Results each. Forty minutes per Objective, two and a half hours total — about two-thirds the time of a standard retreat, with KRs that the room owns.

## When this works and when it doesn't

The exercise works best when the Objective is already chosen. Picking the Objective is harder than picking the KRs and is where the half-day retreat actually earns its time. If you start a 5-in-5 with the Objective unsettled, you'll get five Key Results for five different Objectives. Spend the morning aligning on Objectives. Spend the afternoon doing 5-in-5 sessions for each.

The exercise also requires a small enough room. With more than six people, the read-aloud round runs over. Cap at six. If you have more leaders than that, run two parallel sessions and reconcile.

The exercise does not replace the 1:1 conversations the CoS has with each function lead about what the function actually plans to do. Those still happen. The 5-in-5 just makes sure the *shape* of the goals is honest before the conversations begin.
